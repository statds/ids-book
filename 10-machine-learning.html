10  Machine Learning

The models in Chapter 9 include logistic and Poisson regression for 311 request outcomes. These are statistical models, and they are also examples of supervised machine learning when the goal is to predict a known outcome from features. Statistics and machine learning overlap; they differ more in their emphases than in a clean boundary. Statistical modeling often emphasizes a specified relationship, uncertainty, and interpretation. Machine learning often emphasizes predictive performance and procedures that learn patterns from data. Both fields develop methods for explanation and prediction (James et al., 2023).

This chapter places the models from Chapter 9 in a broader map of learning methods. It introduces common learning tasks, explains why performance on new data matters, and shows how tuning and pipelines fit into model development. The worked examples in Chapter 9 demonstrate model fitting, score calculation, tuning, and prediction evaluation.

10.1 Learning Settings

Learning methods differ partly in the feedback available during learning. Let \(X\) denote input features and, when an outcome is observed, let \(Y\) denote that outcome. These symbols describe the information available to a learning procedure; they do not determine which method is appropriate or what conclusions its results support.

Supervised learning uses examples with known outcomes to fit a rule that predicts outcomes for new cases. Classification predicts a category, such as whether a request closes within a stated time. Regression predicts a numeric quantity, such as a count or duration. Logistic regression in Chapter 9 is a classification method; the Poisson model is used there for a count outcome.

Unsupervised learning looks for structure when the analysis has no specified outcome to predict. Clustering assigns observations to groups based on their features, and dimension reduction represents many features with fewer quantities. These methods can help explore data, but a cluster is not automatically a real-world kind of person or event. The result depends on which observations and features are included, how they are measured, and how the method is configured.

Reinforcement learning concerns a sequence of decisions. An agent observes a state, chooses an action, and receives a reward after interacting with an environment. The aim is to learn a policy, or rule for choosing actions, that performs well over time. A reward is not simply a label attached to each record: actions can change later states and rewards. This chapter introduces the setting but does not develop reinforcement-learning algorithms (Sutton & Barto, 2018).

These three settings provide a useful introduction, but they do not cover every learning design. For example, semi-supervised and self-supervised methods use additional structures in the data. The right method depends on the question, available feedback, and intended use of the result.

10.2 Models and Algorithms

A model is a representation of a relationship or a rule for producing predictions. A learning algorithm is the procedure that uses data to fit a model. The distinction matters because different algorithms can fit models for the same task, and a single algorithm can produce different fitted models when its data or settings change.

Fitting a model estimates parameters from training data. In logistic regression, the fitted coefficients are parameters. A hyperparameter controls how the fitting procedure works rather than being estimated as an ordinary model coefficient. The C setting in scikit-learn’s regularized logistic regression controls the strength of regularization; Chapter 9 tunes it using cross-validation. A hyperparameter can change model complexity and predictive performance (scikit-learn Developers, 2026b).

Statistical models remain central to machine learning. A generalized linear model can estimate interpretable associations and make predictions. A more flexible algorithm can capture patterns that a simple model misses, but its predictions may be harder to explain and can be more sensitive to the training data. The task determines which properties matter: predictive accuracy, interpretability, uncertainty, computational cost, or a combination of them.

10.3 Generalization

A model is useful for prediction when it performs well on cases beyond those used to fit it. This ability is called generalization. Training performance describes how well a fitted model matches the data used for fitting; it does not by itself measure performance on future or otherwise unseen cases.

Model complexity can create two different problems. An overly simple model may miss structure in the data and perform poorly even on its training observations. This is underfitting. A highly flexible model may match the training observations closely, including chance variation that will not repeat in new data. This is overfitting. Training error can keep decreasing as flexibility increases, even while predictive error on new data increases.

The bias–variance tradeoff gives one account of why complexity affects prediction. For a new numeric outcome independent of training data \(D\), suppose \(Y=f(x)+\varepsilon\) at a fixed feature value \(x\), where \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid x]=0\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon\mid x)=\sigma^2\). If \(\widehat{f}_D(x)\) is a prediction from a model fitted to \(D\), then its expected squared prediction error can be decomposed as

\[ \mathbb{E}_{D,Y\mid x}\left[ \{Y-\widehat{f}_D(x)\}^2 \right] = \left\{\mathbb{E}_D[\widehat{f}_D(x)]-f(x)\right\}^2 + \operatorname{Var}_D\left[\widehat{f}_D(x)\right] + \sigma^2, \]

where the first term is squared bias, the second is variance across possible training samples, and the final term is irreducible outcome variation under these assumptions. A model with high bias tends to be too restrictive; a model with high variance changes substantially when its training data change. Greater flexibility can reduce bias while increasing variance, but the balance depends on the data and the learning procedure. The decomposition is a result for squared-error prediction under stated assumptions, not a universal formula for every task. For classification and unsupervised learning, the same words are sometimes used as intuition, but the decomposition above does not directly apply (James et al., 2023).

10.4 Evaluation and Tuning

Evaluation estimates how well a fitted model performs for the cases that matter to its intended use. The data split should reflect that use. A random split may be unsuitable when predictions concern future time periods, unseen locations, or groups whose observations are dependent. In those settings, a temporal or grouped split may give a more relevant assessment.

Training, validation, and test data serve different purposes. Training data fit model parameters. Validation data compare candidate models or hyperparameter settings. A test set is held aside for a final assessment after the model and its settings have been selected. When data are limited, cross-validation repeatedly divides the training data into fitting and validation folds. Its score summarizes performance across those folds for a chosen metric. The score depends on the split design and does not guarantee performance under a different population or deployment setting (scikit-learn Developers, 2026a).

Tuning chooses hyperparameters, such as regularization strength or tree depth. Clustering methods also have settings, such as the number of clusters, that require criteria suited to an unsupervised task. The tuning criterion should match the task and the costs of errors. In Chapter 9, cross-validation selects logistic regression’s C by mean log loss, and an untouched test set assesses the selected model. The chapter also explains why using the same validation results both to select and report final performance can make the reported score optimistic (Varma & Simon, 2006).

No single score answers every question. Accuracy summarizes the fraction of correct classifications at a specified threshold, while log loss assesses predicted probabilities and ROC AUC assesses ranking across thresholds. Numeric outcomes require other measures, such as mean absolute error or root mean squared error. The score should be interpreted with the baseline, the prediction setting, and the consequences of errors in view (scikit-learn Developers, 2026c). The 311 example in Chapter 9 works through these distinctions.

10.5 Pipelines

A modeling pipeline connects data preparation steps to a final estimator. For example, a classification pipeline may impute missing values, encode categorical variables, scale numeric variables, and then fit a classifier. The fitted pipeline applies the same sequence to new records when producing predictions. In scikit-learn, a Pipeline or make_pipeline object represents such a sequence; a ColumnTransformer can apply different preparation steps to different columns (scikit-learn Developers, 2026d).

Keeping preparation in the pipeline is essential during cross-validation. Each fold must learn quantities such as scaling means, imputation values, or category encodings from that fold’s training records. It then applies those learned transformations to the validation records. If the entire data set determines a transformation before the split, information from validation records can affect fitting and make the score too optimistic. The example in Chapter 9 demonstrates a pipeline with mixed variable types and shows how it works inside cross-validation.

A pipeline chains data transformations and a final estimator. Ensembles and stacking combine predictions from multiple fitted models; they are different algorithmic constructions. This chapter focuses on pipelines that combine preprocessing steps with an estimator, as in Chapter 9.
