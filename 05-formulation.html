5  Problem Formulation

Problem formulation connects a domain purpose, available evidence, and a question the evidence can address. This chapter develops that connection through New York City’s 311 service requests directed to the New York City Police Department (NYPD) during the week containing Labor Day 2026. The available extract is the starting point: its categories and quality checks guide the choice and refinement of an illegal-parking question. Domain context and documentation determine what the resulting analysis can establish.

Before running this chapter’s code, update the local book source and its Python environment so that both match the current version. From the repository directory, pull the latest changes, activate the book’s .ids environment, and install the pinned requirements:

git pull
source .ids/bin/activate
make install

On Windows, activate .ids/Scripts/activate from Git Bash. Keep the environment active while running the chapter’s code.

5.1 Introduction

Problem formulation can begin with a domain problem or an available dataset, but either route must connect a substantive purpose to evidence capable of addressing it. Administrative records are often created to operate a service, then made available for analysis before any research question has been chosen. Their categories, times, locations, and unusual values can suggest questions that an investigator would not have formulated in advance. In this chapter, the NYPD extract’s distribution of reported problems motivates illegal parking as a working topic; a domain purpose and account of what these service requests measure determine whether a resulting question is worth pursuing.

The question-to-data route begins with a concern and seeks suitable evidence. A transportation planner might ask where blocked bicycle lanes are reported and then search for records with the necessary location and problem fields. The data-to-question route begins by inspecting records and asking which observed variation warrants investigation. Both routes require iteration: questions direct further inspection, and the meaning and limitations of the data can change the questions. A pattern noticed while inspecting data remains exploratory until domain knowledge, alternative explanations, and further evidence have been considered. Yu & Barter (2024) describe this movement among questions, data, and analysis as a nonlinear data science life cycle. The present example begins with an administrative extract that was collected for service operations rather than for the proposed comparison. The analyst must therefore establish a domain purpose for any pattern found in the file and revise the question if the recorded fields do not represent the desired concept. This sequence keeps the data-to-question route connected to the intended use of an answer.

The first activity in the data-science workflow aligns three components:

  1. Domain purpose: Why would an answer matter, and who might use it?
  2. Question: What description, comparison, prediction, or decision is sought?
  3. Data: What was observed, how was it measured, and what setting does it represent?

This alignment makes the \(D\) in the \(SDC^3\) formulation operational. Domain knowledge gives recorded values their meaning and identifies comparisons of interest. Statistics clarifies which claims the observations can support. Computing enables acquisition and inspection, while collaboration and communication expose assumptions that one investigator might overlook.

A candidate question becomes researchable when its unit, outcome, place, period, comparison, and intended claim are explicit. For this chapter, the broad topic of illegal parking can be narrowed to NYPD-directed 311 service requests, their reported problem details and closure times, and the week containing Labor Day 2026. The provisional question is: among NYPD-directed illegal-parking requests from this week, how did recorded closure-time distributions vary across reported problem details? The comparison is exploratory because the groups will be selected after inspecting the records, and it will require revision if the detail labels or closure timestamps do not measure the intended concepts. Each restriction should serve a domain purpose because the resulting conclusion applies only to the selected records.

Four complementary checks assess whether a candidate question is worth pursuing and whether the available evidence can answer it.

  • Domain fit asks whether an answer would inform a substantive concern or decision, and who could use it. A data-first question needs a domain purpose just as a problem-first question needs evidence suited to its purpose.
  • Measurement fit asks whether the recorded variables represent the concepts in the question. For example, the NYPD records measure time to administrative closure; without further documentation, they do not measure time to an officer’s arrival.
  • Scope fit asks whether the observational unit, population, place, and period match the intended claim. This extract contains NYPD-directed requests from one week, not every parking condition in New York City.
  • Evidence fit asks whether the study design supports the proposed descriptive, comparative, predictive, or causal claim. A difference in recorded closure times can describe these requests, but it does not by itself show that problem type caused a difference.

These checks are iterative rather than a one-time screen. A domain concern may lead to a data search, while an observed pattern may suggest a new question; in either direction, checking measurement, scope, and evidence can require revising the question. The sections that follow evaluate the provisional comparison against the downloaded records and refine it in light of what those records can establish.

5.2 Interpreting the NYPD 311 Records

The observational unit in this chapter is one New York City 311 service request directed to NYPD. The public dataset records requests routed to responding agencies, and its Unique Key field identifies rows (NYC Open Data, n.d.). A row is neither an independently verified violation nor necessarily a unique street condition: several requests may concern the same situation, while other conditions may never be reported. Request counts therefore measure activity recorded by this service system within a defined scope. A location with few requests may have fewer conditions, less use of 311, or both; the table alone cannot separate those explanations. The identifier must be present and unique if each row is to represent one request in subsequent counts. The downloaded records must also be checked against the stated agency and time restrictions before the illegal-parking problem filter defines the working subset.

The timestamp fields define an administrative interval rather than a direct measure of field response. Created Date records the creation of a request, and Closed Date records its closure by the responding agency; their difference is the recorded closure time. An officer could arrive before that closure, and the reported condition could end at another time. The archive does not contain observations of those events, so relabeling the difference as response or resolution time would change the measured concept without evidence. The timestamps in this extract are local New York clock times without an explicit timezone. The selected week does not cross a daylight-saving transition, which avoids one source of ambiguity in subtracting its recorded times but does not validate each timestamp. An analysis of individual reporting behavior would additionally require information about reporters that the public table does not provide.

Variable roles depend on the question and the geographic concept being represented. Borough can define broad comparison groups, while Problem Detail (formerly Descriptor) distinguishes reported categories within illegal parking. Unique Key identifies a request and Incident Zip identifies a postal area; their digits are labels rather than measured magnitudes and should be imported as text. Latitude and longitude locate a recorded incident point, whereas a police precinct code refers to an administrative unit. A coordinate, precinct, and ZIP label can disagree or be missing because they are recorded through different fields and describe different spatial objects. These distinctions govern later preparation: an area merge can use a ZIP label, but a point-to-precinct distance requires a usable coordinate, a precinct location, and a defined distance metric. Treating all geographic fields as interchangeable would conceal both coverage limits and the units of the resulting analysis.

5.3 Acquiring and Importing Data

Data acquisition obtains evidence while preserving enough information to interpret and reproduce it. Common sources include local files, public repositories, open-data portals, application programming interfaces (APIs), databases, surveys, experiments, sensors, and administrative systems. The method should match the source and intended use. A stable file is convenient for a fixed classroom or research example, while an API or database query is more appropriate when an analysis must refresh regularly.

A reproducible acquisition records the source and the intended selection rules. The example file came from the NYC Open Data portal, where we attempted to download NYPD requests created during the week containing Labor Day 2026. The portal identifier erm2-nwe9 locates the full 311 dataset and can be used to find its documentation or construct an API request; it is a source identifier, not a request-level key. The archive contains the returned records but not a saved copy of the portal query settings, so the intended filters and the delivered values must be checked separately.

The delivered file does not match the intended time window: it contains 1,902 records created during the first eight hours of September 13. This discrepancy shows why a successful download is not evidence that the portal returned exactly the requested data. The intended agency restriction also requires an independent check against the Agency values in the file.

Raw and derived data serve different purposes. The downloaded source extract preserves what the provider returned. The smaller illegal-parking data frame will be derived in code by applying the intended time restriction and then a problem-category filter while retaining all 44 source columns. Preserving the source makes it possible to audit or revise both filters. The source file should not be edited manually to remove the extra records.

The book distributes the downloaded CSV in a ZIP archive:

data/311_nypd_lbdwk_2026.csv.zip

The archive contains one CSV file and reduces its size by about 90% without changing the tabular source representation. Pandas can decompress a single-member ZIP archive during import. The filename identifies the service, agency, period, and year without depending on spaces or punctuation that require shell quoting. A companion provenance record should carry details that cannot fit sensibly in a filename, including the direct source, access date, requested selection rules, and observed departure from those rules.

5.3.1 Importing the Archive

Reproducible import begins with a project-relative path. A relative path works after another reader clones or moves the project because it describes the file’s location inside the project rather than its location on one person’s computer. The Path class also provides portable operations for checking and measuring the file.

from zipfile import ZipFile

import pandas as pd

data_path = Path("data") / "311_nypd_lbdwk_2026.csv.zip"

if not data_path.exists():

The import specification should express meaning that cannot be inferred safely from the characters in the file. The two identifiers are read as strings, and the large mixed table is read in one pass before the date fields are converted with an explicit format.

identifier_types = {
    "Unique Key": "string",
    "Incident Zip": "string",
}

requests = pd.read_csv(
    data_path,
    compression="zip",
    dtype=identifier_types,
    low_memory=False,
)

date_format = "%m/%d/%Y %I:%M:%S %p"
for column in ["Created Date", "Closed Date"]:
    requests[column] = pd.to_datetime(
        requests[column],
        format=date_format,
        errors="coerce",
    )

The explicit datetime format documents the month-day-year order, 12-hour clock, seconds, and morning or afternoon marker. The errors="coerce" argument converts an invalid timestamp to a missing datetime rather than silently leaving a mixture of strings and dates. That choice makes conversion failures countable, but it does not make them harmless; every failure should be investigated.

5.4 Checking Data

Basic checks test whether the imported object matches its stated scope before any values are cleaned or transformed. They do not prove that every record is correct. Instead, they turn assumptions about the extract into results that can be reviewed. The governing principle is to trust the provider while verifying every condition on which the analysis depends.

The first checks address identity, agency, and both temporal boundaries. A portal selection is an instruction about what to return; these checks test whether the delivered records satisfy it. For the Sunday-through-Saturday week, the intended creation-time window begins at midnight on September 6 and ends immediately before midnight on September 13. The inclusive lower bound and exclusive upper bound include every timestamp on the seven intended days without ambiguity at the next week’s boundary.

start = pd.Timestamp("2026-09-06")
end = pd.Timestamp("2026-09-13")

valid_created = requests["Created Date"].notna()
in_intended_week = requests["Created Date"].between(
    start, end, inclusive="left"
)
agency_mismatch = requests["Agency"].isna() | ~requests["Agency"].eq("NYPD")
before_start = valid_created & requests["Created Date"].lt(start)
on_or_after_end = valid_created & requests["Created Date"].ge(end)
outside_intended_week = before_start | on_or_after_end

scope_checks = pd.Series({
    "rows delivered": len(requests),
    "missing unique keys": requests["Unique Key"].isna().sum(),
    "duplicated unique keys": requests["Unique Key"].duplicated().sum(),
    "agency mismatches": agency_mismatch.sum(),
    "agency values": requests["Agency"].nunique(dropna=False),
    "invalid created dates": requests["Created Date"].isna().sum(),
    "created before week": before_start.sum(),
    "created at or after next week": on_or_after_end.sum(),

This one-week illegal-parking subset contains no creation or closing timestamps exactly at midnight. Only four creation times and ten closing times fall exactly on an hour, and four closure durations equal a whole number of hours. Its absence does not invalidate a problem found in a larger or different extract; it shows why a quality report must identify the agency, variables, and time period checked.

5.5 Efficient Representation

Data representation affects storage, import time, type preservation, and interoperability. The CSV format is widely supported text that is easy to inspect with ordinary tools. Its simplicity is valuable for acquisition and archival exchange, but each reader must parse the text and reconstruct such types as datetimes and identifiers.

Apache Arrow defines a column-oriented representation designed for efficient analytical data interchange across systems. Feather Version 2 stores Arrow’s interprocess communication format in a portable file and supports typed columns and compression (Apache Arrow Developers, 2026). Bates and Yan describe how an Arrow representation can create a unified dataset for efficient analysis across software platforms (Bates & Yan, 2024).

A derived Feather file can preserve the imported table’s typed representation for repeated analysis. The example writes to a temporary directory so that rendering the chapter does not add a generated file to the project.

from tempfile import TemporaryDirectory

with TemporaryDirectory() as temporary_directory:
    feather_path = Path(temporary_directory) / "nypd_download.feather"
    requests.to_feather(feather_path)
    requests_from_feather = pd.read_feather(feather_path)

    representation = pd.DataFrame({
        "format": ["CSV", "ZIP-compressed CSV", "Feather"],
        "size_mib": [
            csv_member.file_size / 2**20,
            data_path.stat().st_size / 2**20,
            feather_path.stat().st_size / 2**20,
        ],
    })

representation.round({"size_mib": 2})

The ZIP archive is smaller than the Feather file in this example. Efficient representation does not mean smallest file under every criterion: ZIP applies strong general-purpose compression to text, while Feather preserves a typed, column-oriented representation designed for analytical access. Storage size, read time, type preservation, and cross-platform use should therefore be compared separately.

The round trip should preserve the shape, values, and important types. Separate checks make failures easier to diagnose than one large comparison whose false result gives no explanation.

pd.Series({

The temporary file demonstrates the conversion without creating an output during book rendering. A persistent copy for later analysis should record both filters, the source archive, and the conversion choices in its provenance.

Performance comparisons require more care than a single stopwatch reading. Operating-system caching, storage hardware, background activity, parsing options, and compression can change the result. A fair comparison repeats each operation, reports a robust summary such as the median, and includes the CSV type conversions needed by the actual analysis. The exercises ask readers to design and document that comparison.

Efficient formats complement rather than automatically replace source formats. The CSV member of the ZIP archive remains a broadly readable text artifact. A Feather file is useful as a typed derivative or interchange artifact when the same table is loaded repeatedly or used by multiple analytical systems. Both need provenance because a compact or compressed file does not explain where its values came from or why its rows were selected.

5.6 Provenance and Responsibility

Data provenance records the history needed to interpret and reproduce a dataset. A useful record names the provider, source, access date, selection rules, observational unit, temporal scope, transformations, and known limitations. It also distinguishes statements made by the provider from decisions made by the analyst.

File integrity can be recorded with a cryptographic hash. A matching hash shows that two copies contain the same bytes; it does not show that the data are accurate or appropriate for a question.

import hashlib

with data_path.open("rb") as input_file:
    sha256 = hashlib.file_digest(input_file, "sha256").hexdigest()

Responsible acquisition also limits unnecessary exposure. This public dataset does not identify the person who submitted a request, but it contains precise incident addresses and coordinates. An analysis that needs only ZIP Code or precinct summaries should avoid displaying individual addresses. Public availability does not eliminate the responsibility to minimize unnecessary detail and to follow the source’s terms of use.

Open-data curation requires more than distributing a readable file. Tussey and Yan propose six principles from their study of NYC 311: cross-organizational consistency, accurate data entry, efficient storage, current data dictionaries, automated quality assurance, and transparency (Tussey & Yan, 2025). Their examples include inconsistent representations of missing values, invalid ZIP Codes, implausible date relationships, unusual concentrations at particular times, redundant fields, and categories whose meaning varies by agency or period.

These findings are prompts for investigation rather than universal cleaning rules. A negative duration may reveal a timestamp error, a misunderstood field, or a clock convention. A concentration at midnight may be a default, a batch process, or a genuine operational schedule. A responsible analyst first quantifies the pattern, checks documentation and related fields, and asks the data curator when the intended meaning remains uncertain. Deleting or replacing an unusual value without that inquiry can hide the very process that needs to be understood.

The first and last questions can be investigated directly as descriptions of this NYPD extract. The operational questions require care because closure is an administrative event, not necessarily a field response. The safety and equity questions require the most augmentation: request counts alone cannot reveal how often a condition occurred, how many people were exposed, or how likely different communities were to use 311. The extract cannot support comparisons with agencies absent from the file.

Domain knowledge also helps choose a useful level of detail. “Where is illegal parking a problem?” is too broad because neither where, problem, nor the relevant measure has been defined. A narrower question might ask, “During the Labor Day week, which precincts recorded the most requests about blocked fire hydrants, and did their median closure times differ?” The revision identifies the unit, category, place, period, and comparison, while still acknowledging that the result describes recorded requests rather than all blocked hydrants.

A final evaluation applies the domain, measurement, scope, and evidence checks to the proposed claim rather than merely to its computational form. The records may show that requests created at night have different closure times from those created during the day, but the comparison does not establish an effect of reporting time: problem detail, location, staffing, and reporting behavior may also vary with the hour. Similarly, averaging ZIP Codes after they have been imported as numbers is computationally possible but has no geographic interpretation. Raw request counts by ZIP label describe the file, yet they are not comparable as per-resident reporting rates when areas differ in population and parking opportunities. Each proposed analysis must therefore name its measured quantity, eligible requests, comparison basis, and intended inference. A causal claim would additionally require a design and assumptions that address competing explanations.

Additional area-level data could supply denominators and context for ZIP-level request counts. Census characteristics are often tabulated for ZIP Code Tabulation Areas (ZCTAs), which are related to postal ZIP Codes but do not represent identical geographies (U.S. Census Bureau, 2023). Section 6.5 demonstrates one such enrichment and evaluates its key, reference period, join cardinality, and unmatched records. Even a validated match describes places rather than the individuals who submitted requests.

5.8 A Working Formulation

A working formulation records the current alignment of purpose, target, population, and evidence while allowing later checks to revise it. The eligible population consists of NYPD-directed illegal-parking requests created during September 6–12, 2026; the outcome is each request’s recorded creation-to-closure interval in hours. The three most frequently reported problem details are Blocked Hydrant, Posted Parking Sign Violation, and Blocked Sidewalk. For a detail group \(g\), the descriptive target is the distribution of those closure intervals among eligible requests labeled \(g\), rather than a causal effect of the label or a citywide incident rate. Selecting groups by their observed frequencies avoids choosing them because their closure summaries appear different, but the selection still uses the same week under study. These definitions yield the working comparison:

Among NYPD illegal-parking requests created from September 6 through September 12, 2026, how did recorded closure-time distributions differ among the three most frequently reported problem details?

The working comparison has a defined unit, period, outcome, and group rule, but its interpretation remains descriptive. Groups were selected after inspecting the week’s category frequencies, and the records were collected through a reporting system rather than a probability sample of parking conditions. Location, reporting time, and agency practices may also differ among the categories. A difference between their closure-time distributions would therefore characterize these recorded requests during a holiday week; it would not identify the effect of changing a request’s problem label. Exploratory analysis can examine distribution shape and subgroup variation, while a later study could define a broader target period or collect process data to investigate the operational reasons for a difference. The present formulation makes those later requirements visible before a summary statistic or plot is chosen.

Other features of the checked extract support different formulations. The problem-detail table records 3,711 requests about blocked hydrants and 379 about blocked bike lanes, while 99 illegal-parking requests lack coordinates. These observations suggest several questions with distinct uses:

  1. Which precincts recorded the most blocked-hydrant requests during the week, and how did their closure-time distributions differ?
  2. Within each problem detail, did requests created at night have longer closure times than requests created during the day?
  3. Were requests without coordinates concentrated in particular boroughs or problem details, and would their omission change a geographic summary?
  4. How would the geographic pattern of blocked-bike-lane requests change if counts were compared with bicycle-lane mileage or another exposure measure?

The first three questions can be investigated within this NYPD extract, though the data cannot explain the cause of a difference without further evidence. The fourth requires an additional geographic dataset and a defensible match between its areas and the request locations. Each question concerns recorded requests rather than all conditions on the street.

The data-to-question route remains open after these examples. What other questions do the screened records suggest? For each candidate, identify the observational unit, measurements, comparison, and claim that the available evidence could support. State what additional data or domain knowledge would strengthen the answer.
