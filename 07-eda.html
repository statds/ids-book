7  Exploratory Data Analysis

Exploratory data analysis (EDA) examines distributions, relationships, and irregularities before a final account of findings is written. Its purpose is to learn what the observed data can show, what they cannot show, and which questions deserve closer investigation (National Institute of Standards and Technology, n.d.). This chapter first states principles that apply across datasets and then develops them through a case study. The next chapter develops principles for choosing and evaluating visual displays across the data-science workflow; here, plots support investigation alongside numerical summaries and checks of the records.

7.1 EDA Principles

EDA asks what patterns and irregularities are present in a prepared dataset, and which questions deserve more investigation. Three principles guide the work:

  1. Explore a question, not a plot collection. Preparation checks in Chapter 6 establish whether identifiers are unique and joins preserve rows. EDA then asks how an outcome is distributed, how groups differ, and which observations merit inspection. The same count can serve different purposes: missing coordinates may be a preparation diagnostic, while differences in missingness by borough may be an EDA question about which requests a map leaves out (Tussey & Yan, 2025; Yu & Barter, 2024).
  2. Make comparisons fair. Center, spread, tails, missingness, and group size describe different features. A mean alone cannot describe a long right tail. Counts from two weekend days and five weekdays have different exposure, and copying one area income value onto many requests does not create many independent area observations. Choose summaries and plots for the comparison, then check that they tell a consistent story (National Institute of Standards and Technology, n.d.).
  3. Separate description from inference. A table can summarize every record in the defined scope. A test or interval asks what might happen beyond those records and therefore adds assumptions. Those assumptions need special care when the groups or thresholds were selected after examining the data. A result may also change under a plausible alternative time window, treatment of long durations, or geographic grouping. Such sensitivity checks show whether a conclusion depends on one analysis choice, but cannot repair undercoverage or confounding (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016; Yu & Barter, 2024).

In practice, EDA cycles through a question, a calculation, an inspection, and a possible revision. A surprising value may send us back to the source; a group difference may suggest a better denominator or a more focused question. The calculation itself does not determine whether a pattern is important. Interpretation still depends on the eligible records, how the measurements were made, and what the analysis can support.

7.2 Study Setup

The case study uses the screened requests from Chapter 6 to revisit the question in Chapter 5. The analysis proceeds in this order:

  1. Describe the full distribution of recorded closure hours.
  2. Compare the three most frequently reported problem-detail groups. The frequency rule is set before examining their closure summaries, but it does not make the groups random or comparable in other respects.
  3. Examine variation by detail, creation date, borough, and matched area to see what a citywide median might hide.
  4. Use a permutation calculation and bootstrap interval to illustrate how inference adds assumptions, then check sensitivity to selected analysis choices.

At each step, the four formulation checks in Section 5.1 limit what the observed differences can establish.

The initial scope report provides an audit of the table that enters this analysis rather than a second lesson in import and cleaning. The shared preparation function applies the documented time and problem filters, timestamp parsing, field names, and closure-time definition from Chapter 6. The report then verifies the number of request identifiers, missing or negative outcomes, and missing coordinates that affect later spatial comparisons. These checks establish the eligible set for the present figures; they do not resolve whether reporting behavior or agency practice differs across groups. The order of analysis is deliberate: inspect the overall distribution, apply the frequency-based group rule, examine time and place, and only then consider inferential and sensitivity calculations. This order makes it possible to distinguish a comparison specified by the working formulation from an additional pattern noticed during exploration.

7.3 Closure Time by Detail

The overall closure-time distribution establishes which summaries can represent a typical request and which features require separate attention. The median divides observed intervals into lower and upper halves, while a quantile identifies a specified fraction of intervals at or below its value. The mean reflects every recorded duration and is more sensitive to the long upper tail. Reporting these quantities together allows readers to distinguish a typical interval from delayed closures rather than reducing both to one number. The table below calculates summaries from every eligible closure interval. The accompanying histogram displays only the first 24 hours on its horizontal axis to make the dense part of the distribution legible. That visual limit is not a row exclusion from the table or subsequent comparisons; the caption and text must make the difference clear. A later sensitivity calculation explicitly applies a 24-hour row restriction and reports its effect.

The median closure interval is about 1.67 hours, while the mean is about 3.51 hours. The 90th percentile is about 8.73 hours, the 99th percentile is about 23.58 hours, and the maximum is nearly 200 hours. Read these summaries together:

  • The median describes the middle request: half of recorded intervals are shorter and half longer.
  • The mean is larger because it is pulled upward by the long right tail.
  • The upper percentiles and maximum show that a small share of requests took much longer than the typical request.

Thus, “requests close in about two hours” is incomplete. A large interval could be valid, a timestamp correction, or an operational exception; its size alone does not show which. The group comparison therefore reports medians and quartiles alongside means.

The group rule uses request frequency, not closure time, to choose categories. The code calculates each selected group’s count, center, spread, and upper quantile while retaining the provider’s original category labels. Similar labels are not combined without evidence that they share an operational meaning. A different week could produce a different list of frequent categories.

The empirical cumulative distribution provides a second way to compare the groups. At a closure time \(t\), its vertical value is the fraction of requests closed by time \(t\). For example, a value of 0.70 at \(t=2\) hours means that 70% of the group’s recorded requests closed within two hours. The curves allow comparisons at any threshold without assuming that the distributions have the same shape. Since both the group rule and analysis were developed using this week, later inferential calculations remain exploratory.

7.4 Variation Across Groups

Temporal and geographic stratification examines whether a pooled group contrast combines different request mixes. Creation date identifies when a report entered the service system, whereas closure time is the subsequent elapsed interval; grouping by creation date does not assign a request to the date on which it closed. The first table reports the daily volume of eligible requests, so each row has a one-day observation period. The second table counts the three selected problem details by borough and shows whether their relative frequencies differ across places. These tables are descriptive checks on composition, not estimates of the frequency of illegal parking on the street. A difference in group mix can help explain why a citywide closure-time summary differs from a within-borough summary, but the tables alone do not establish the agency process that generated either pattern.

Subgroup composition can also change the direction of an overall comparison. Simpson’s paradox describes cases where an association in aggregated data weakens or reverses after data are divided into subgroups defined by a third variable (Simpson, 1951). The following hypothetical example illustrates the arithmetic with treatment success rates. Treatment A has a higher success rate than treatment B in both risk groups, but A is used much more often for high-risk cases. The pooled rates therefore favor B. The values are constructed for instruction and do not describe a medical study.

simpson = pd.DataFrame({
    "risk": ["Lower", "Lower", "Higher", "Higher"],
    "treatment": ["A", "B", "A", "B"],
    "successes": [81, 234, 192, 55],
    "cases": [87, 270, 263, 80],
})
simpson["success_rate"] = simpson["successes"] / simpson["cases"]
within_risk = simpson.pivot(
    index="risk", columns="treatment", values="success_rate"
)
case_shares = simpson.pivot(
    index="risk", columns="treatment", values="cases"
)
case_shares = case_shares.div(case_shares.sum(axis=0), axis=1)
pooled = simpson.groupby("treatment")["successes"].sum() / simpson.groupby(
    "treatment"
)["cases"].sum()
result = pd.concat([within_risk, pooled.rename("Pooled").to_frame().T])
result["A case share"] = case_shares["A"]
result["B case share"] = case_shares["B"]
result.loc["Pooled", ["A case share", "B case share"]] = 1
result.round(3)

The table makes the changing weights visible: treatment A has a larger share of its cases in the higher-risk group, where success is less common for both treatments. The pooled row summarizes success rates, while the case-share columns show each treatment’s composition.

This reversal does not show which treatment is more effective. Risk group may be related to treatment assignment, and other differences may remain within each stratum. Analysts should report the overall and within-group summaries and explain why the stratifying variable matters before drawing a causal conclusion. The later visualization chapter develops small-multiple displays that make subgroup comparisons easier to inspect (Armstrong & Wattenberg, 2014). It applies this approach with facet_grid() (see Chapter 8).

7.5 Uncertainty Assessment

A hypothesis test can be part of EDA when its assumptions and limited purpose are explicit. Consider the median closure-time difference between Blocked Hydrant and Blocked Sidewalk requests. For the observed week, that difference is descriptive and needs no test. A test addresses a different question: under a model in which requests from the two detail groups have exchangeable closure-time distributions, how unusual would the observed difference be? The permutation calculation below pools the two groups and reallocates their labels while preserving their sizes. Its two-sided statistic is the absolute difference between medians. A bootstrap interval separately resamples requests within each group to show variability under an independent-request model.

The appropriate test depends on the outcome scale, comparison structure, and sampling or assignment process. The table links common questions to Python functions and states the null or design condition each function addresses (SciPy Developers, 2026; statsmodels Developers, 2026b, 2026a). This guide is not an automatic decision rule: inspect the data and define the target contrast before choosing a test (National Institute of Standards and Technology, n.d.).

Question and design Test and Python function Null or key condition
Is one numerical mean equal to a reference value? One-sample \(t\) test, scipy.stats.ttest_1samp Population mean equals the reference value.
Do two independent groups have different means? Welch \(t\) test, scipy.stats.ttest_ind(..., equal_var=False) Population means are equal; equal variances are not assumed.
Do paired measurements have different means? Paired \(t\) test, scipy.stats.ttest_rel Mean within-pair difference is zero; pairs are independent.
Do three or more independent groups have equal means? One-way ANOVA, scipy.stats.f_oneway; use equal_var=False for Welch ANOVA All group means are equal; classical ANOVA assumes equal variances, and follow-up comparisons need multiplicity control.
Are two categorical variables associated? Chi-square test, scipy.stats.chi2_contingency Variables are independent; the large-sample approximation must be adequate.
Is a sparse \(2\times2\) table associated? Fisher exact test, scipy.stats.fisher_exact Tests association conditional on fixed table margins.
Does one proportion equal a benchmark, or do two proportions differ? Binomial test, scipy.stats.binomtest; two-proportion test, statsmodels.stats.proportion.proportions_ztest Proportions equal the stated benchmark or each other; samples must follow the test’s independent-sampling design.
Do paired binary outcomes change? McNemar’s test, statsmodels.stats.contingency_tables.mcnemar The two paired marginal proportions are equal; pairs are independent.
Are two numerical variables associated? Pearson, scipy.stats.pearsonr; Spearman, scipy.stats.spearmanr Tests zero linear correlation or zero rank correlation, respectively.
Do independent groups differ in rank distributions? Mann–Whitney, scipy.stats.mannwhitneyu; Kruskal–Wallis, scipy.stats.kruskal Tests equal distributions or rank locations under their assumptions; neither automatically tests equal medians.
Is a chosen statistic unusual under exchangeable labels? Permutation test, scipy.stats.permutation_test Labels can be reassigned under the null; the statistic must match the question.

The first code example uses the contingency table from the three most frequent details to ask whether the observed detail mix varies by borough. The chi-square test evaluates independence, while Cramér’s \(V\) summarizes the strength of the association. The expected cell counts help assess whether the chi-square approximation is adequate. Because the details were selected by frequency in this same week, this is an exploratory calculation, not a confirmatory test of a prespecified hypothesis.

The next example uses Welch’s test to compare arithmetic mean closure hours for hydrant and sidewalk requests. It answers a different question from the median comparison below, and the long right tail can make the mean sensitive to a small number of long intervals. The request-level calculation also assumes independent observations, although requests may share locations, times, or administrative conditions.

Requests from one week may share time, location, or agency conditions, which weakens a simple independence model. A statistical test does not correct those design limitations, and selecting a test after searching many groupings can make its nominal \(p\)-value misleading (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016). Rank-based tests such as Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis do not automatically test a difference in medians. Likewise, a bootstrap interval estimates uncertainty under a resampling scheme; it is not itself a hypothesis test. When many outcomes, subgroups, or alternatives are examined, report that search and do not treat a threshold such as 0.05 as a mechanical decision rule (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016).

hydrant = top.loc[
    top["problem_detail"].eq("Blocked Hydrant"), "closure_hours"
].dropna().to_numpy()
sidewalk = top.loc[
    top["problem_detail"].eq("Blocked Sidewalk"), "closure_hours"
].dropna().to_numpy()
observed_gap = np.median(hydrant) - np.median(sidewalk)

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
bootstrap_gaps = np.array([
    np.median(rng.choice(hydrant, size=len(hydrant), replace=True))
    - np.median(rng.choice(sidewalk, size=len(sidewalk), replace=True))
    for _ in range(2000)
])
interval = np.quantile(bootstrap_gaps, [.025, .975])
combined = np.concatenate([hydrant, sidewalk])
permuted_gaps = np.array([
    np.median(shuffled[:len(hydrant)])
    - np.median(shuffled[len(hydrant):])
    for shuffled in (rng.permutation(combined) for _ in range(2000))
])
extreme = np.count_nonzero(np.abs(permuted_gaps) >= abs(observed_gap))
p_value = (extreme + 1) / (len(permuted_gaps) + 1)
pd.Series({
    "observed median gap (hours)": observed_gap,
    "bootstrap lower endpoint (hours)": interval[0],
    "bootstrap upper endpoint (hours)": interval[1],
    "permutation p-value": p_value,
}).to_frame("value").round(4)

The observed median gap is about 0.865 hours, and the bootstrap interval is approximately 0.712 to 1.016 hours. The estimated permutation \(p\)-value is about 0.0005, with the added one in the numerator and denominator preventing a reported zero from a finite set of permutations. These results show that the observed separation is large relative to these relabelings and resamples. They do not establish that the detail label causes a closure-time difference. Requests may be related through place, time, repeat reporting, or agency practice, and the analysis was chosen after inspection of this week. The bootstrap interval does not include uncertainty from coverage, measurement, or group selection. A \(p\)-value should not be read as the probability that the null hypothesis is true or as a measure of practical importance (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016).

Sensitivity analysis quantifies how much a descriptive result changes under defensible alternatives to the primary calculation. Yu and Barter emphasize that choices made during cleaning, preprocessing, and analysis can affect a reported finding (Yu & Barter, 2024). The first check below restricts closure intervals to at most 24 hours to assess the influence of the long upper tail; it does not classify longer intervals as invalid. The remaining checks calculate the same hydrant-minus-sidewalk median gap within each borough, holding the outcome definition and group labels fixed while changing geographic composition. These are targeted perturbations of the eligible set rather than an exhaustive search for a desired result. The table reports group sizes alongside gaps because a large contrast based on few requests deserves a different interpretation from a similar contrast based on many. Neither sensitivity check removes confounding or extends the conclusion beyond the observed week.

def median_gap(frame):
    left = frame.loc[
        frame["problem_detail"].eq("Blocked Hydrant"), "closure_hours"
    ]
    right = frame.loc[
        frame["problem_detail"].eq("Blocked Sidewalk"), "closure_hours"
    ]
    return pd.Series({
        "hydrant_requests": len(left),
        "sidewalk_requests": len(right),
        "median_gap_hours": left.median() - right.median(),
    })

sensitivity = pd.DataFrame({
    "all requests": median_gap(top),
    "closure at most 24 hours": median_gap(
        top.loc[top["closure_hours"].le(24)]
    ),
}).T
for borough in sorted(top["borough"].dropna().unique()):
    sensitivity.loc[borough.title()] = median_gap(
        top.loc[top["borough"].eq(borough)]
    )
sensitivity[["hydrant_requests", "sidewalk_requests"]] = sensitivity[[
    "hydrant_requests", "sidewalk_requests"
]].astype(int)

Staten Island 54 34 2.635

The all-request median gap is about 0.865 hours and changes little, to about 0.848 hours, after intervals above 24 hours are excluded. The geographic comparisons are less uniform: the gap is about 1.07 hours in Brooklyn and 1.09 hours in Queens, but only 0.10 hours in the Bronx and approximately zero in Manhattan. Staten Island has a larger gap, but its comparison uses only 54 hydrant and 34 sidewalk requests. Group sizes and possible differences in case mix or processing limit the interpretation of these contrasts. The pooled result therefore does not describe every borough equally well.

Exploration has answered the initial descriptive question for the recorded week: closure-time distributions differ among the three most reported details, with a long right tail and the largest median among Blocked Hydrant requests. It has also identified conditions for a more focused next analysis. A future question could compare those details within borough and creation-time strata across multiple weeks, while documenting changes in request volume and closure practices. A causal question about why closure times differ would require a design that addresses confounding and the meaning of the recorded outcome. The ZCTA analysis similarly motivates area-level questions but does not turn area income into a request-level explanation. EDA makes observed patterns and the limits of their interpretation explicit enough to guide that next question. ## Exercises {.unnumbered}
