9  Modeling

Predictive modeling uses information available at a defined moment to estimate an outcome that is not yet known. The preceding chapters developed a question, prepared request-level data, examined the closure-time distribution, and visualized variation across time and place. This chapter carries that work forward with a domain question: can information available when an illegal- parking request is created help identify requests that will remain open for at least one hour? It examines whether request detail and borough, together with neighborhood population density, household vehicle availability, and income from the Census-linked table, improve predictions. The analysis uses the same one-week records and request-level observational unit as the earlier chapters. It does not claim that an area characteristic causes a request to take longer, or that one week represents future requests under changed conditions.

The one-hour outcome connects the modeling question to the operational concern that illegal-parking reports may call for timely handling. It is an analytical cutoff, not a documented agency response standard. In the available week, 34.6% of requests with usable closure times were closed in under an hour, while 65.4% remained open for at least an hour. Recorded closure time is only a proxy for handling time: it may include dispatch, travel, queueing, reporting, and administrative steps. The analysis can predict this recorded outcome from observed fields, but it cannot isolate those processes.

9.1 Frame the Prediction

A prediction is useful only when its target, prediction time, available features, and intended decision are clear. Exercise 7.1 examined a two-hour cutoff as an exploratory summary. Here, the analysis uses a one-hour cutoff to ask a more time-sensitive question. Reconsidering a threshold for a new analytical purpose is reasonable when the change is stated and its effects are visible; the new threshold should not be described as a policy rule.

The observational unit is one illegal-parking request in the distributed week of data. The target equals one when its recorded closure time is at least one hour and zero otherwise. Requests with missing closure time cannot be labeled and are excluded from this supervised analysis. The candidate features in the main model are problem detail and borough, recorded when the request is created, plus population density, median household income, and the share of households with a vehicle available from the Census-linked area table (U.S. Census Bureau, 2024a, 2024b). Population density divides the ACS population estimate by 2024 Gazetteer ZCTA land area. The vehicle share is one minus the estimated fraction of households with no vehicle available. These measures describe area context rather than curb capacity or individual behavior. The ACS estimates cover 2020–2024, precede the request week, and have sampling uncertainty; the derived vehicle share’s uncertainty is not calculated from the published margins of error. The incident ZIP label serves as the link to area context, as described in Section 6.4; a later benchmark also treats it as a categorical identifier. The lookup supplies 2020–2024 American Community Survey (ACS) estimates for ZIP Code Tabulation Areas (ZCTAs). A ZCTA and a postal ZIP Code are related but distinct geographies, so the link is an approximation rather than an exact address-level match (U.S. Census Bureau, 2023, 2026). The ZIP digits themselves are not used as a quantitative predictor. Closure time and any field that records later processing are excluded because they would not be available at prediction time.

The prediction question is: among requests from the same observed week and with similar recorded fields and area context, how well can a model distinguish requests that remain open for at least an hour? This question describes prediction within the data’s observed setting. It does not ask whether borough, problem detail, population density, household vehicle availability, or income causes a longer closure, and it does not establish how a model would perform in another week. The four formulation checks in Section 5.1 remain relevant: a sound model cannot repair an unclear target, an invalid measurement, an inadequate scope, or weak evidence.

9.2 Establish a Baseline

A baseline sets a reference that a more complex model must improve. Because about two-thirds of eligible requests meet the one-hour target, predicting the majority class for every request already gives roughly 65% accuracy. That result does not distinguish requests: it labels every case as taking at least an hour. Accuracy alone would therefore make a useless classifier look respectable.

The analysis starts from the shared preparation function rather than repeating the import and filtering workflow from Chapter 6. It links the requests to the Census context already documented in Section 6.4, reports unmatched and incomplete records, and defines the binary outcome. It does not alter the source request data or rebuild the Census lookup. The train-test split reserves one quarter of complete requests for a final evaluation. Stratification keeps the target proportion similar in the two sets; it does not make the records independent or simulate a future week.

from scripts.parking_requests import attach_zcta_context, load_parking_requests

The checks make the analysis population and target visible before any model is fitted. The lookup matches 13,033 of 13,040 requests; 58 matched requests lack at least one area measure, including nine requests with no household vehicle estimate because their ACS household denominator is zero. 9.3 Use a GLM

A generalized linear model (GLM) relates a response distribution to a predictor through a link function. For a binary outcome, the response is modeled with a Bernoulli distribution and a logit link; this special case is logistic regression. GLMs also include familiar linear regression with a Gaussian response and identity link, as well as count models with a Poisson response and log link. The response distribution and link should follow the outcome’s scale and support, rather than being selected only because a software function is familiar (McCullagh & Nelder, 1989; Nelder & Wedderburn, 1972).

For request (i), let (Y_i=1) indicate that closure takes at least one hour, and let (p_i = (Y_i=1 _i)). Logistic regression models the log odds as a linear combination of predictors:

[ !() = _0 + 1 x{i1} + + k x{ik}. ]

The inverse-logit transformation maps any real-valued linear predictor to a probability between zero and one. A coefficient describes a change in log odds, holding the other included predictors fixed. Exponentiating a coefficient gives an odds ratio, but an odds ratio is not a probability difference and does not by itself describe a causal effect.

The first GLM includes problem detail, borough, population density, median household income, and household vehicle availability. The three area measures are standardized across training requests, so their odds ratios describe a one-standard-deviation difference in the request-weighted feature values. The formula interface compares each categorical level with a reference category and holds the other predictors fixed. These coefficients describe conditional associations in the fitted model, not changes caused by the area characteristics (statsmodels Developers, 2026).

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

glm_data = X_train.copy()
glm_data["long_closure"] = y_train
glm_formula = (
    "long_closure ~ C(problem_detail) + C(borough) + "
    "standardize(population_density_per_sqmi) + "
    "standardize(median_household_income) + "
    "standardize(share_households_with_vehicle)"
)
glm_fit = smf.glm(
    glm_formula,
    data=glm_data,
    family=sm.families.Binomial(),
).fit()

population_density_log_odds = glm_fit.params[
    "standardize(population_density_per_sqmi)"
]
income_log_odds = glm_fit.params["standardize(median_household_income)"]
vehicle_share_log_odds = glm_fit.params[
    "standardize(share_households_with_vehicle)"
]
pd.Series({
    "Population-density odds ratio per standard deviation": np.exp(
        population_density_log_odds
    ),
    "Income odds ratio per standard deviation": np.exp(income_log_odds),
    "Household vehicle-share odds ratio per standard deviation": np.exp(
        vehicle_share_log_odds

The odds ratios summarize adjusted associations in this training split, with each area measure compared across a one-standard-deviation difference. The context values repeat across requests in the same ZCTA, so area-level patterns are supported by the number of distinct ZCTAs, not by treating every request as an independent area. These estimates do not describe individual residents or causal effects. They also depend on the model’s assumption that each standardized measure has a linear relationship with log odds.

9.4 Check the Model

Model checks ask whether the chosen specification represents the observed relationships adequately. They differ from test-set evaluation: a model can fit the training records poorly, or fit them closely while predicting new records poorly. Logistic regression does not assume normally distributed errors or constant error variance as ordinary least squares does. Its assumptions instead concern the conditional Bernoulli response and the specified relationship between predictors and log odds. Residual and influence diagnostics can reveal unusual records or lack of fit, but no single diagnostic proves that a model is correct (Hosmer et al., 2013; Pregibon, 1981).

The current model assumes a linear relationship between each standardized area measure and log odds. It also imposes an additive structure: the detail contrast is the same across boroughs unless an interaction is included. Sparse categories can make estimates unstable. Checks should compare observed outcome rates with fitted probabilities across boroughs and across ordered groups of each continuous measure, while retaining each group’s count. Because the Census values repeat within ZCTAs, the 176 distinct areas also limit how much independent evidence supports an area-level pattern. Calibration matters because a probability estimate of 0.7 should correspond to an outcome rate near 0.7 among comparable predictions (Calster et al., 2019).

The training-set summary below compares observed target rates with average fitted probabilities by borough. A large discrepancy can flag a lack of fit for a group, while close averages do not rule out errors for individual requests or interactions hidden within a borough. This is an in-sample check for the simple GLM, not an estimate of future prediction accuracy.

9.5 Evaluate Predictions

Evaluation asks how well the fitted model predicts requests it did not use for estimation or tuning. A training score is optimistic because the model has already adapted to those records. The reserved test set is used only after model choice; repeatedly adjusting a model after inspecting its test performance turns that set into another training resource and weakens the evaluation. Here, even a clean random holdout supports only a limited claim: it estimates performance for additional requests drawn under a similar one-week data-generating process.

Different metrics answer different questions. Accuracy counts correct classifications at a chosen probability threshold. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC AUC) evaluates ranking across all possible thresholds: 0.5 indicates no ability to rank positive cases better than chance, and 1 indicates perfect ranking. ROC AUC does not select a threshold or show whether predicted probabilities are accurate. Log loss evaluates the probabilities themselves and penalizes confident errors (scikit-learn Developers, 2026c).

Threshold-based measures describe different parts of the confusion matrix. A true positive (TP) is a request that takes at least an hour and is flagged as such; a false negative (FN) takes at least an hour but is not flagged. A false positive (FP) closes sooner than an hour but is flagged, while a true negative (TN) closes sooner and is not flagged. Different fields often use different names for the same ratio. The following table gives common names and makes each denominator explicit (scikit-learn Developers, 2026c; Wikipedia contributors, n.d.).

Quantity Common names Definition
True positive rate TPR, sensitivity, recall, hit rate, probability of detection \(TP/(TP+FN)\): fraction of actual positives flagged
False negative rate FNR, miss rate \(FN/(TP+FN)\): fraction of actual positives missed
True negative rate TNR, specificity, selectivity \(TN/(TN+FP)\): fraction of actual negatives not flagged
False positive rate FPR, fall-out, false alarm rate \(FP/(TN+FP)\): fraction of actual negatives flagged
Positive predictive value PPV, precision \(TP/(TP+FP)\): fraction of flagged requests that are positive
False discovery rate FDR \(FP/(TP+FP)\): fraction of flagged requests that are negative
Negative predictive value NPV \(TN/(TN+FN)\): fraction of unflagged requests that are negative
False omission rate FOR \(FN/(TN+FN)\): fraction of unflagged requests that are positive
Accuracy Overall accuracy, correct classification rate \((TP+TN)/(TP+TN+FP+FN)\)
F1 score F1 measure, F-score with \(\beta=1\) \(2\,\mathrm{Precision}\,\mathrm{Recall}/(\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall})\)

Sensitivity and specificity are common in diagnostic testing, while precision and recall are common in information retrieval and machine learning. The names reflect different traditions, but the calculations match when the same class is designated positive. Recall conditions on the actual positives; precision conditions on the predicted positives. F1 is the equal-weight harmonic mean of precision and recall, so it does not account for true negatives. TPR and FNR sum to one because they share the actual-positive denominator; TNR and FPR, PPV and FDR, and NPV and FOR also form complementary pairs. These names and denominators are summarized in the Wikipedia overview of binary-classifier evaluation, while the scikit-learn guide documents the corresponding software metrics (scikit-learn Developers, 2026c; Wikipedia contributors, n.d.). Other F-scores change the relative weight of precision and recall.

These measures depend on the classification threshold and the relative costs of missed long closures and unnecessary alerts. The data do not specify those costs, so a threshold of 0.5 is used below only as an illustration. If a decision threshold were selected for use, that choice would be made with training or validation data and then evaluated once on the held-out test set.

The baseline assigns every request the training-set target prevalence as its probability and predicts the majority class. The Census-context model one-hot encodes problem detail and borough, standardizes the three area measures, then fits logistic regression. The test metrics compare the probability model with that baseline and report classification at a 0.5 threshold. Because the positive class is more common, accuracy should be read alongside ROC AUC, log loss, and the confusion matrix rather than on its own.

from sklearn.dummy import DummyClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import (
    accuracy_score,
    confusion_matrix,
    f1_score,
    log_loss,
    precision_score,
    recall_score,
    roc_auc_score,

from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import OneHotEncoder, StandardScaler

candidate_features = categorical_features + numeric_features
preprocessor = ColumnTransformer([
    ("categorical", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), categorical_features),
    ("numeric", StandardScaler(), numeric_features),
])
baseline = DummyClassifier(strategy="prior").fit(
    X_train[candidate_features], y_train
)
baseline_probability = baseline.predict_proba(X_test[candidate_features])[:, 1]

model = make_pipeline(
    preprocessor,
    LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000),
)
model.fit(X_train[candidate_features], y_train)
test_probability = model.predict_proba(X_test[candidate_features])[:, 1]

def score_predictions(probability):
    predicted_class = probability >= 0.5
    return {
        "accuracy": accuracy_score(y_test, predicted_class),
        "ROC AUC": roc_auc_score(y_test, probability),
        "log loss": log_loss(y_test, probability),
        "TPR / recall": recall_score(y_test, predicted_class),
        "specificity": recall_score(y_test, predicted_class, pos_label=0),
        "precision": precision_score(y_test, predicted_class),
        "F1": f1_score(y_test, predicted_class),
    }

This first comparison gives the model a common reference and an initial held-out score. Its results are not the final model-selection result because the regularization strength still needs to be chosen using training data alone. A 0.5 classification threshold is convenient for illustration, not a universal decision rule. A real alerting system would set a threshold by considering the consequences of each kind of error with the people who would use the predictions.

9.6 Regularize Logistic Regression

Regularization adds a penalty to the fitting objective to limit coefficient size and reduce sensitivity to features that fit quirks in the training records. Ridge-style (L_2) regularization shrinks coefficients toward zero; lasso-style (L_1) regularization can set some coefficients exactly to zero. Elastic net combines the two penalties (Tibshirani, 1996; Zou & Hastie, 2005). The code below uses the (L_2) penalty and compares stronger with weaker shrinkage on the same held-out requests.

Scikit-learn’s logistic regression applies (L_2) regularization by default. Its C parameter is the inverse penalty strength, so smaller values apply stronger shrinkage (scikit-learn Developers, 2026b). The analyst should select C using only the training data. The pipeline places one-hot encoding inside each fold, which prevents the validation records in a fold from influencing category processing or model fitting. Cross-validation estimates how candidate settings perform across several training/validation splits (scikit-learn Developers, 2026a); using the same cross-validation scores both to select and to report a final error can be optimistic, which is why the separate test set remains untouched until selection is complete (Varma & Simon, 2006).

The comparison separates measured area context from a postal-code lookup. All four models use the same training and test requests. One model uses problem detail and borough; a second adds three ACS-derived area measures; a third adds the incident ZIP label as a categorical identifier; and a fourth uses a smoothed target encoding of that label. Five-fold stratified cross-validation selects C by log loss, a probability-focused criterion. Standardization and one-hot encoding are inside the pipeline so each fold learns its preprocessing from its own training portion. The ZIP-label model is a predictive benchmark for areas already represented in the data; its coefficient for a ZIP label does not explain what neighborhood feature may relate to closure time.

One-hot encoding gives each ZIP label its own coefficient, which can be unstable when some labels have few requests. Smoothed target encoding offers another representation: it replaces a label with an estimate of its one-hour closure rate, then shrinks that estimate toward the overall rate. where \(s_j\) is the number of one-hour closures in ZCTA \(j\), \(n_j\) is its number of requests, \(\bar{p}\) is the overall training-set rate, and \(\alpha\) controls shrinkage. Larger values of \(\alpha\) pull area estimates more strongly toward the overall rate. This empirical Bayes idea is useful for categories with different sample sizes (Micci-Barreca, 2001; Pargent et al., 2022). Since the encoding uses the outcome, fitting it on each row’s own outcome would leak information into the training data. The encoder below cross-fits the training values within each training fold; for the held-out test set, it uses category rates estimated from the full training set (scikit-learn Developers, 2026d).

from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV, StratifiedKFold
from sklearn.preprocessing import TargetEncoder

cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=2026)
candidate_C = {"logisticregression__C": [0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10]}

def tune_logistic(categorical, numeric):
    transformers = []
    if categorical:
        transformers.append((
            "categorical",
            OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"),
            categorical,
        ))
    if numeric:
        transformers.append(("numeric", StandardScaler(), numeric))
    encoder = ColumnTransformer(transformers)
    pipeline = make_pipeline(
        encoder,
        LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000),
    )
    search = GridSearchCV(
        pipeline,
        candidate_C,
        scoring="neg_log_loss",
        cv=cv,
        refit=True,
    )
    features = categorical + numeric
    search.fit(X_train[features], y_train)
    probability = search.predict_proba(X_test[features])[:, 1]
    return search, probability

zip_identifier_features = categorical_features + ["incident_zip"]
detail_borough_search, detail_borough_probability = tune_logistic(
    categorical_features, []
)
census_search, census_probability = tune_logistic(
    categorical_features, numeric_features
)
zip_id_search, zip_id_probability = tune_logistic(
    zip_identifier_features, []
)

    ColumnTransformer([

For this split, adding the Census measures changes test ROC AUC from about 0.577 to 0.589 and log loss from 0.636 to 0.631. The improvement is modest, but it indicates that the supplied area attributes add some predictive information beyond problem detail and borough in this test design. The ZIP-label model scores higher, with ROC AUC about 0.690 and log loss 0.593. Because the same ZCTAs occur in training and test sets, this score reflects area-specific prediction for locations represented in training. It neither identifies a cause nor demonstrates transfer to a new ZCTA or a later week.

Smoothed target encoding gives a similar test ROC AUC of about 0.688 and log loss of 0.595, with the cross-validation procedure selecting a smoothing value of 10. Three test requests have ZIP labels absent from the training set; the encoder assigns those labels the overall training rate. This comparison shows that a compact, partially pooled encoding can retain much of the ZIP-specific predictive signal in this split. It does not establish that ZIP-level outcome rates will transfer to future requests, and its learned category values should not be interpreted as neighborhood causes.

The Census estimates offer a more interpretable area-level question than the ZIP label, but they do not measure the mechanisms that directly govern parking or request processing. Population density is residents per square mile, not curb or parking-space density. Household vehicle availability is not ownership, registration, vehicle activity, or parking location, and median household income does not describe an individual requester. These ACS measures repeat across requests in the same ZCTA and carry sampling uncertainty; the vehicle share’s uncertainty has not been propagated. Only 176 ZCTAs contribute independent area contexts in this analysis. Direct measures of curb regulations, parking supply, vehicle activity, or precinct workload would be more informative for those processes. Any resource- allocation use would also require a separate review of measurement, fairness, and consequences for affected neighborhoods.

Regularization controls model complexity but does not replace diagnosis. The selected penalty can stabilize estimates while leaving omitted variables, measurement error, unfair performance differences, and domain changes unresolved. Area-level predictors also require validation that respects their shared geography when the intended use concerns new areas. The model’s intended use and validation design must therefore be considered together.
